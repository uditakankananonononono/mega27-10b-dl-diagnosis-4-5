I will judge these as an ISEF computational-biology project pair, not as “can this produce a paper-like result.” The key distinction is:

A benchmark correction / leakage audit can be scientifically valuable, but it is usually weaker than a genuine predictive advance unless you demonstrate that the corrected benchmark changes conclusions broadly.

A “beats published baseline” claim is only defensible if the baseline is reimplemented or evaluated under the exact same split, preprocessing, and metric.

For ISEF, a discovery cannot simply be “our model is better”; it needs a biological or clinical insight extracted from the model.

Candidate A — PCOS diagnosis (Kaggle PCOS, n=541, 41 features)
1. Is the benchmark-beat claim defensible?

Partially, but not in the form “we beat the 100% model.”

The 100% accuracy result is almost certainly not a meaningful ceiling. If feature selection and SMOTE-ENN occur before the train/test split, the test set is contaminated. A leakage-free reproduction could legitimately show that the published number collapses.

However:

“We exposed leakage and achieved higher accuracy than the published 100% baseline” is not defensible.

A judge will immediately ask:

“How can anything beat 100%?”

The correct framing is:

“We perform the first leakage-controlled reassessment of PCOS ML on this benchmark and show that previous performance estimates are inflated; our model achieves the best performance under a preregistered leakage-free protocol.”

That is a methods contribution, not a normal benchmark victory.

The bigger problem

The dataset itself is weak for an ISEF flagship.

Problems:

Issue	Why judges care
n=541	small tabular dataset
Kaggle-derived	unclear clinical provenance
41 features	many models will memorize patterns
single cohort	no external validation
hormonal features	diagnosis already encoded biologically

A GNN/patient graph architecture is unlikely to add much. With 541 patients, graph edges may mostly encode demographic similarity rather than biology.

Could it still work?

Yes, but only if the discovery angle is strong.

A better contribution would be:

“Leakage-resistant interpretable PCOS phenotyping reveals clinically meaningful subgroups.”

Examples:

Cluster patients into metabolic vs reproductive PCOS phenotypes.

Identify whether hormonal markers predict diagnosis independently of anthropometric variables.

Use SHAP/Integrated Gradients + stability analysis across bootstrap samples.

But as a diagnosis model benchmark project, I would not choose it.

Verdict:

Scientific validity: ★★★★☆

Benchmark potential: ★★☆☆☆

ISEF strength: ★★☆☆☆

Candidate B — Alzheimer's disease from OASIS-1 MRI
1. Is the benchmark-beat claim defensible?

Much more defensible than PCOS, but still problematic.

OASIS-1:

416 subjects

structural MRI

dementia labels

public and widely used

The problem:

Many published CNN results of 90–99% are not comparable.

Common issues:

slice-level leakage (multiple slices from same patient in train/test)

subject leakage

small test sets

heavy augmentation

non-independent validation

A fair project could absolutely expose this.

But a CPU-only 2D CNN competing against MRI deep-learning papers claiming 95%+ is unlikely to produce a clean “beat.”

Your likely outcome:

Proper subject-level split → performance drops.

Your model may be competitive.

But “state-of-the-art” is unlikely.

The stronger angle

Do not sell it as diagnosis.

Sell:

“Can lightweight interpretable MRI representations identify early neurodegenerative signatures under subject-independent validation?”

Possible discoveries:

Which brain regions contribute most?

Can a small model recover known Alzheimer’s anatomical patterns?

Does graph construction from regional embeddings reveal disease progression?

The issue:

OASIS-1 is old. Many judges know it.

Verdict:

Scientific validity: ★★★★☆

Benchmark potential: ★★★☆☆

ISEF strength: ★★★☆☆
